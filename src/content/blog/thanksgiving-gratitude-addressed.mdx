Thanksgiving is Thursday, November 26. It's the one day a year explicitly organized around gratitude, and it's remarkable how much of that gratitude never reaches anybody.

The standard practice is to go around the table and name what you're thankful for. It's a good ritual. But notice what it is: gratitude described, in a room, mostly about people and circumstances not present. It's a private state, said out loud to a third party.

Gratitude that lands somewhere is a different object. It has an address on it.

### The unsent-thank-you problem

Nearly everyone is carrying a few of these. The teacher who changed the direction of your life and doesn't know it. The friend who took your call at two in the morning during the worst year. The relative who covered something for you when you were twenty-three and never brought it up again.

The reasons they stay unsent are always the same and always weak: too much time has passed, they probably already know, it would be weird now.

They usually don't already know. That's the part people get wrong most consistently. The person who mattered to you rarely has any idea of the scale of it, because your side of that story was never told to them.

### Thanksgiving is the socially cheapest week to do it

The advantage of the holiday isn't the turkey — it's that it's the one week of the year when an out-of-nowhere message of gratitude requires no explanation. Any other week, "I've been thinking about what you did for me in 2019" is a slightly alarming text. In late November it's just the season.

If you've got one of these sitting around, this is the low-friction moment to clear it.

### And for the family that isn't in the room

Thanksgiving is also the holiday most defined by who couldn't make it — the sibling in another time zone, the grandparent in a care facility, the branch of the family that alternates years. A card that several people sign is a way for the table to reach the people who aren't at it.
