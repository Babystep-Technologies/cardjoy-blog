Customer Service Week runs Monday, October 5 through Friday, October 9. Most companies mark it with catered lunch, which is fine, and with a slide that says "our support team is the heart of our company," which is usually not.

The thing worth naming about support work is the emotional arithmetic. Nobody contacts support when things are going well. Every conversation starts at some level of frustration, and the person answering has to walk in already regulated, already patient, already calm — twenty, forty, sixty times a day. The interactions are individually small and cumulatively heavy.

And the reward structure is inverted. Handle a furious customer perfectly and the outcome is a customer who is merely no longer furious. Nobody writes a case study about a problem that stopped being a problem.

### The data everyone has and nobody uses

Support is one of the most heavily instrumented functions in a company. CSAT scores, response times, resolution rates, verbatim customer comments. All of it sits in a dashboard.

Which means the raw material for genuinely specific appreciation is already sitting there, and it mostly gets used for reporting rather than for telling someone what they did well. The customer who wrote three paragraphs about how a rep saved their launch — that comment lives in a spreadsheet cell. It could live in a card.

### Quote it back

If you're recognizing a support team this week, the single highest-leverage move is to go find the actual words a customer used and give them to the person they were about. Not aggregated. Not "our CSAT was up 4 points." The sentence itself.

"A customer wrote in October that you were the first person at any vendor who'd ever called them back when you said you would." That's a thing someone carries around.

### Peers see things managers don't

The other source worth mining is the team itself. Support reps know exactly who takes the escalations nobody wants, who writes the macros everyone else uses, who stays calm on the call that would have gone badly with anyone else on it. That knowledge almost never gets said out loud.
